One good number hides your worst failures

1 min read

The model had a great overall score. The customer still said it didn’t work.

Both were true.

A single metric like mAP averages across everything in your test set. If most of your data is clear daytime footage of large, close objects, that’s what the number mostly measures. The hard cases, like night, rain, glare, far range and small objects, are a small share of the frames. They barely move the average.

But they’re exactly the cases operators notice. Nobody complains about the thousand easy detections. They remember the one miss at 2 AM in the rain.

What changed how my teams evaluate models:

  • Break every evaluation into slices that match real conditions: time of day, weather, distance, object size, camera, location
  • Report each slice separately, and put the worst one at the top of the report, not the average
  • Weigh slices by business impact, not by how many frames they happen to have
  • Gate releases on the worst slice. A new model that improves the average but gets worse at night doesn’t ship

The average tells you how the model does on your dataset. The slices tell you how it does in your customer’s world.

Ship on your worst slice, not your average.
